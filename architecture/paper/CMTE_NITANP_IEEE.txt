The Capacity Illusion: A Leakage-Audited 85-Run
Benchmark of 21 Model Conﬁgurations for
Romanized Telugu–English Sentiment Analysis
First A. Author, Student Member, IEEE, Second B. Author, Member, IEEE, and Third C. Author, Senior
Member, IEEE

Abstract—Code-mixed Romanized Telugu–English (Tenglish)
is a low-resource, orthographically noisy sentiment task. CMTENITANP, a 34,588-comment corpus, is a standard benchmark, yet
published comparisons mix random and duplicate-aware splits,
different tokenizers, and different tuning budgets. We present a
leakage-audited benchmark of 21 model conﬁgurations and 85
ﬁnal runs on a group-disjoint split (Split B) of CMTE-NITANP.
The matrix spans 11 classical learners, three deep networks,
and seven transformer conﬁgurations: MuRIL (LoRA and full
ﬁne-tuning), plus LoRA for mBERT, Distil-mBERT, XLM-R,
IndicBERTv2, and TeluguBERT. The best transformer, fully
ﬁne-tuned MuRIL (238 M parameters), reaches 83.36 macroF1; a TF-IDF logistic regression with no pretrained parameters
(16,892 word unigrams, 50.7 K coefﬁcients) reaches 82.93, and
its word+character union reaches 83.24 in a single-seed ablation.
The full-ﬁne-tuning gap is +0.0043 in mean macro-F1 and is
not statistically signiﬁcant (paired-bootstrap ∆ = +0.0039, 95%
CI [−0.0062, +0.0140], McNemar p = 0.44, Holm p = 0.82).
Parameter count does not predict accuracy (Spearman ρ = 0.306,
p = 0.504). A random split contains 345 exact Jaccard≥ 0.8
train–test pairs and 203 test rows with a near-duplicate in train;
the group-disjoint split contains zero. Feature ablation shows
that the signal is unigram-lexical (word bigrams are worse),
and WordPiece tokenization does not close the gap. Under
orthographic perturbation, vowel elongation and combined noise
cause the largest drops; zero-shot transfer to CMTET loses 0.18–
0.26 macro-F1. We argue that the transformer advantage on this
task is largely a capacity illusion: once leakage, tokenization,
and tuning are controlled, a lexical linear model is statistically
indistinguishable from a 238 M-parameter model at a small
fraction of the cost. Splits, code, predictions, logs, and ﬁgures
are provided in the project repository for reproduction.
Index Terms—Code-mixed sentiment analysis, Telugu–
English, benchmark, data leakage, linear models, transformers,
parameter-efﬁcient ﬁne-tuning, reproducibility.

I. I NTRODUCTION
ODE-MIXING—the alternation of two or more languages within a single utterance—is the normal register
of multilingual social media. In Telugu–English code-mixing,
often written in Roman script (Tenglish), speakers mix English
words with Romanized Telugu morphology (e.g., “movie chala
bagundi” ‘the movie is very good’). Sentiment analysis of
such text is difﬁcult for three reasons: the text is short, spelling
is highly variable, and the same sentiment may be expressed
through Telugu, English, or a hybrid construction.

CMTE-NITANP [2] is a widely used 34,588-comment
Telugu–English sentiment corpus with three classes (negative,
neutral, positive). Recent work reports that transformer models
improve over classical baselines by about 5% accuracy and 6%
F1 on this corpus [2]. That claim is plausible, but it is difﬁcult
to evaluate: the corpus contains substantial near-duplication,
and reported experiments differ in split construction, tokenizer,
and tuning budget. On a small, noisy, lexical-cue-driven task,
each of these choices can move macro-F1 by an amount
comparable to the reported transformer advantage.
This paper asks a deliberately narrow question: on a leakfree split, with tuning held ﬁxed, does a 238 M-parameter
transformer actually beat a linear model on CMTE-NITANP?
We answer it with a controlled benchmark of 21 conﬁgurations
and 85 ﬁnal runs, spanning classical learners, deep networks,
LoRA adapters, and full ﬁne-tuning. We also audit the split
itself with exact Jaccard matching, because near-duplicate
leakage is the most common explanation for inﬂated socialmedia results [23].
A. Research Questions
RQ1 (ranking): Which model family attains the best
macro-F1 on a leak-free split?
• RQ2 (linear vs. transformer): Is the best transformer
signiﬁcantly better than the best linear model?
• RQ3 (capacity): Does parameter count predict macro-F1
across the matrix?
• RQ4 (robustness): How much does orthographic noise
degrade each model?
• RQ5 (cost): What accuracy is obtained per second of
training?
• RQ6 (calibration): Which models produce reliable conﬁdence estimates?
• RQ7 (transfer): How well do models transfer zero-shot
to CMTET?
• RQ8 (abstention): Does conﬁdence-based abstention buy
a useful risk–coverage trade-off?

C. Main Findings
On Split B, fully ﬁne-tuned MuRIL is the nominal winner at 83.36 macro-F1 (83.38 accuracy). A TF-IDF logistic regression with 16,892 word unigrams reaches 82.93; a
word+character union reaches 83.24 in a single-seed ablation.
The full-ﬁne-tuning gap is +0.0043 in mean macro-F1 and
is not signiﬁcant (paired-bootstrap ∆ = +0.0039, 95% CI
[−0.0062, +0.0140], McNemar p = 0.44, Holm p = 0.82).
Parameter count is uncorrelated with accuracy (ρ = 0.306,
p = 0.504). LoRA adapters (0.59–1.18 M trainable parameters) all trail the linear model. Word bigrams are worse
than word unigrams, character n-grams are competitive, and
WordPiece tokenization improves a CNN+BiLSTM only from
78.63 to 79.45—far short of closing the gap. We conclude
that the task’s signal is predominantly unigram-lexical, that a
linear model is the correct inductive bias for it, and that the
reported transformer advantage is consistent with an artifact
of leakage, tuning, or both.
II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Code-Mixed Sentiment Analysis
Sentiment analysis of code-mixed Indian social media was
popularized by shared tasks such as SemEval-2020 Task 9
[5] and the DravidianLangTech / DravidianCodeMix series
[4]. Telugu–English resources include CMTE-IIITH [3] and
CMTE-NITANP [2]. Rayala et al. introduced syllable-aware
word embeddings for Telugu–English [1] and later evaluated
transformer models on both corpora [2]. Their abstract reports
a 5% accuracy and 6% F1 improvement over baselines on
CMTE-NITANP. The absolute numbers are reported in the
paper’s Tables 6–7; we treat the relative claim as reported
context and do not quote absolute values that we could not
verify from the accessible record.
B. Transformers and Parameter-Efﬁcient Fine-Tuning
Multilingual encoders—mBERT [6], XLM-R [7], MuRIL
[8], IndicBERT [9], and TeluguBERT—are the default starting

2

point for low-resource Indic tasks. Full ﬁne-tuning updates
all parameters; LoRA [10] freezes the base model and learns
low-rank updates to selected projections, and 8-bit optimizers
[11] make full ﬁne-tuning feasible on small GPUs. Parameterefﬁcient methods are attractive when compute is scarce, but
their capacity is bounded by the rank and target modules.
Whether that bound matters on a short, lexical task is an
empirical question, and it is the question our LoRA-vs-fullﬁne-tuning comparison isolates.
C. Linear and Lexical Baselines
TF-IDF with linear classiﬁers remains a strong baseline for
short-text sentiment [21], [20]. Character n-grams are robust
to spelling variation [22], which is pervasive in Romanized
Telugu. Linear models have high bias and low variance; on
a small corpus with strong lexical cues, this bias can be an
advantage rather than a limitation. Ensemble and boosting
methods (random forests [14], gradient boosting [15], XGBoost [16], LightGBM [17]) are common alternatives, but they
add capacity without adding lexical knowledge.
D. Leakage and Evaluation Rigor
Near-duplicate leakage is a well-known failure mode in
data mining [23]: random splits place near-identical rows
in both training and test, inﬂating estimates. Social-media
corpora are especially vulnerable because retweets, copy-paste
comments, and spelling variants create large near-duplicate
clusters. Group-aware splitting removes this path. For signiﬁcance testing on a single ﬁxed test set, paired McNemar
tests [24] with Holm correction [25] and paired bootstrap
conﬁdence intervals [26] are appropriate; the Nadeau–Bengio
correction [27] is designed for resampled cross-validation
and is not used here. Calibration is measured with expected
calibration error (ECE) and the Brier score [28].
III. DATASET AND L EAKAGE AUDIT
A. Corpus
CMTE-NITANP contains 34,588 Romanized Telugu–
English comments with three labels: negative (12,583), neutral
(10,944), and positive (11,061). Comments are short: mean
10.46 whitespace tokens (median 8, 95th percentile 30, maximum 148) and mean 67.4 characters (median 48). The training
vocabulary contains 41,150 word types; 10.07% of test tokens
are unseen in training. These statistics already suggest that the
task is local and lexical rather than long-range.
B. Preprocessing
We apply a deterministic, signal-preserving pipeline. Unicode is normalized to NFC; HTML entities are unescaped;
URLs and @mentions are replaced by placeholders; the # of
a hashtag is dropped while the word is kept; whitespace is
collapsed; and text is lowercased. Emojis, punctuation, digits,
and character elongations are not removed, because they carry
sentiment and because removing them would confound the
robustness analysis. On top of this, we apply the 9,388-entry
PLD variant→canonical dictionary released with the corpus,

C. Near-Duplicate Clustering and Splits
We cluster the 34,588 rows with MinHash/LSH over word
3-grams (128 permutations, Jaccard threshold 0.8). This yields
33,313 clusters; 2,283 rows (6.60%) belong to a cluster of
size > 1. Among multi-row clusters, 115 (11.41%) contain
conﬂicting labels, covering 314 rows, with mean label entropy
0.113 bits—a small but non-zero annotation-noise ﬂoor.
We construct two splits of identical size (27,670 / 3,459 /
3,459):
• Split A (control, leaky): stratiﬁed random 80/10/10,
ignoring cluster membership. 203 test rows (5.87%) have
a near-duplicate in training.
• Split B (treatment, leak-free): StratiﬁedGroupKFold
with 10 folds, so an entire cluster lands in one split;
whole clusters are then moved between train/val/test to
match Split A’s sizes. 0 test rows have a near-duplicate
in training.
Split B is a genuine regroup, not Split A with twins moved:
19.99% of training rows, 90.32% of validation rows, and
89.65% of test rows change membership relative to Split A. An
independent exact-Jaccard audit conﬁrms the MinHash result:
Split A has 345 train–test pairs with exact Jaccard ≥ 0.8
(maximum 1.0), while Split B has 0. The audit is stable across
thresholds 0.7, 0.8, and 0.9 (Split A leakage 5.81–6.10%,
Split B 0.00%). All experiments below use Split B.
IV. E XPERIMENTAL P ROTOCOL
A. Model Matrix
The matrix contains 21 conﬁgurations in three families:
• Classical (11): logistic regression, linear SVM [18],
multinomial Naive Bayes, k-NN [19], dummy majority,
random forest, extra trees, histogram gradient boosting,
XGBoost, LightGBM, and a soft-voting ensemble of
logistic regression, random forest, extra trees, and histogram gradient boosting.
• Deep (3): TextCNN, BiLSTM, and CNN+BiLSTM,
trained from scratch on a word-level vocabulary built
from the training split (minimum frequency 2), with 100dimensional embeddings, dropout 0.3, Adam, learning
rate 10−3 , batch 128, maximum length 64, and the same
10-epoch/patience-2 schedule.

3

Transformers (7): MuRIL-base (LoRA and full ﬁnetuning), mBERT (LoRA), Distil-mBERT (LoRA), XLMR-base (LoRA), IndicBERTv2 (LoRA), and TeluguBERT
(LoRA).
Support-vector machines with an RBF kernel are excluded:
probability calibration is quadratic and would be prohibitive on
27,670 rows; the linear SVM is retained as the representative
margin-based learner.

B. Search-Once, Freeze, Then Vary Seeds
Classical and deep models are implemented with scikitlearn [13]; transformer models use HuggingFace Transformers
[12] and PEFT. Every hyperparameter is selected once on
the validation split with seed 42, frozen to a JSON ﬁle,
and then reused unchanged for all ﬁnal seeds. Seeds vary
only weight initialization, data order, and dropout masks; they
never change learning rate, batch size, sequence length, LoRA
rank, or epoch budget. This protocol directly addresses a
common benchmark ﬂaw in which “seed runs” silently retune the model. Classical models use ﬁve seeds (k-NN uses
one; deterministic learners have zero variance across seeds);
deep models use ﬁve seeds; LoRA transformers use three seeds
(42–44); MuRIL full ﬁne-tuning uses one seed because of its
53.9-minute cost on the available GPU.
C. Hyperparameters
TF-IDF uses min_df= 2 and sublinear term frequency.
The primary feature is word unigrams (16,892 features); the
ablation adds word bigrams (40,604), character n-grams (2–5,
85,764), and their union (126,368). Linear models use C =
1.0 (selected on validation). Tree ensembles use 300 trees;
boosting grids cover learning rate {0.05, 0.1} and 200–400
iterations.
LoRA uses r = 16, α = 32, dropout 0.1 on the query
and value projections, learning rate 4 × 10−4 , weight decay
0.01, warmup 0.06, batch 16 with gradient accumulation 2
(effective 32), maximum length 64, 10 epochs, patience 2,
minimum 3 epochs, and best-on-validation restoration. MuRIL
full ﬁne-tuning uses learning rate 2 × 10−5 , 8-bit AdamW
[11], batch 8 with accumulation 4 (effective 32), gradient
checkpointing, and the same schedule. The full-ﬁne-tuning
run uses 237,558,531 trainable parameters; LoRA adapters
use 592,131 (MuRIL, mBERT, IndicBERT, TeluguBERT),
887,811 (Distil-mBERT), and 1,182,723 (XLM-R).
D. Metrics and Statistics
The primary metric is macro-F1 on the 3,459-row test
set. We also report accuracy, weighted-F1, MCC, ECE, Brier
score, and risk–coverage curves. Conﬁdence intervals are 95%
paired bootstrap intervals over the test set. Model comparisons use McNemar’s test on the paired correctness vectors,
with Holm–Bonferroni correction across the 20 comparisons
against logistic regression. We do not use the Nadeau–Bengio
correction because there is a single ﬁxed split, not repeated
cross-validation. All 85 ﬁnal runs are recomputed from stored
preds.npz ﬁles; the recomputation produced zero mismatches.

E. Compute
All runs use one NVIDIA RTX 2050 laptop GPU with 4 GB
of memory (3.87 GB usable) and a parallel CPU lane. The
longest run is MuRIL full ﬁne-tuning at 3,231.7 s (53.9 min);

V. R ESULTS
A. Main Leaderboard (RQ1)
Table II reports the ﬁnal leaderboard. MuRIL full ﬁnetuning is nominally ﬁrst at 83.36 macro-F1 (83.38 accuracy,
MCC 0.750). Logistic regression is second at 82.93 (82.91
accuracy, MCC 0.743). Linear SVM and soft voting follow
at 82.31. The six LoRA adapters range from 80.92 (DistilmBERT) to 81.96 (TeluguBERT) and all trail the linear
model. The deep models range from 78.46 (CNN+BiLSTM)
to 79.83 (BiLSTM). The dummy majority classiﬁer obtains
17.78 macro-F1 at 36.37 accuracy, conﬁrming that macro-F1
is the appropriate primary metric for the near-balanced but not
perfectly balanced test set.
B. Linear vs. Transformer (RQ2)
The paired comparison is unambiguous. Full ﬁne-tuning
versus logistic regression gives a mean macro-F1 difference of +0.0043 (paired-bootstrap ∆ = +0.0039), 95%
CI [−0.0062, +0.0140], McNemar p = 0.4367, Holm p =
0.8158. The conﬁdence interval includes zero, and the point
estimate is smaller than the seed-to-seed standard deviation
of several LoRA models. In contrast, 14 of the 20 comparisons against logistic regression are signiﬁcant after Holm
correction; the exceptions are full ﬁne-tuning, MuRIL LoRA,
soft voting, linear SVM, IndicBERT LoRA, and TeluguBERT
LoRA (16 are signiﬁcant at the uncorrected p < 0.05 level).
The result is not that transformers are weak; it is that the

best transformer and the best linear model are statistically
tied. Table III reports all 20 comparisons; bold rows are not
signiﬁcant after Holm correction.
C. Capacity Does Not Predict Accuracy (RQ3)
Across the seven transformer conﬁgurations, parameter
count and macro-F1 are weakly and non-signiﬁcantly correlated (Spearman ρ = 0.306, p = 0.504; Fig. 3). The 136Mparameter Distil-mBERT (0.8092) is within 0.73 points of
the 238M-parameter MuRIL LoRA (0.8165), and the 279Mparameter XLM-R LoRA (0.8103) is below both. Meanwhile
a 50.7K-coefﬁcient linear model (0.8293) beats every LoRA
adapter. Capacity is not the binding constraint; the binding
constraint is whether the model can represent the lexical cues,
and a linear model can.
D. Per-Class Behavior
Full ﬁne-tuning and logistic regression have almost identical
per-class proﬁles: negative 0.8325 vs. 0.8234, neutral 0.8128
vs. 0.8093, positive 0.8555 vs. 0.8552. Neutral is the hardest
class for both, consistent with the absence of a strong lexical
polarity cue. The full-ﬁne-tuning difference is concentrated
in positive recall (0.8617 vs. 0.8490) and neutral F1 (0.8128
vs. 0.8093); on negative recall it is slightly lower (0.8378 vs.
0.8434). The two models make different errors but reach the
same aggregate. The near-identical positive F1 (0.8555 vs.
0.8552) is particularly striking: the 238M-parameter model
buys no measurable improvement on the easiest class.
E. Cost (RQ5)
The compute gap is more than 300×. Logistic regression
reaches 82.93 in 10.4 s; full ﬁne-tuning reaches 83.36 in
3,231.7 s, a 310× cost for +0.43 points. Multinomial Naive
Bayes reaches 81.21 in 0.2 s, the best F1-per-second in the
matrix. The cost–beneﬁt frontier is populated by classical
models up to 10.4 s; the only transformer on it is full ﬁnetuning at 3,231.7 s, whose 0.43-point gain costs 310×.

C. Adaptation Regime: LoRA vs. Full Fine-Tuning
The LoRA/full-ﬁne-tuning contrast is the sharpest mechanistic result. MuRIL LoRA (0.59M trainable parameters)
reaches 0.8165; MuRIL full ﬁne-tuning (237.6M trainable

cannot fully shift MuRIL’s formal-transliteration priors toward
informal social-media spelling, while full ﬁne-tuning can. The
cost of that adaptation is 400× more trainable parameters and
53.9 minutes versus 20.8 minutes for LoRA.
D. Calibration (RQ6)
LightGBM is the best-calibrated model (ECE 0.0132),
followed by histogram gradient boosting (0.0220) and random forest (0.0429). The transformers are poorly calibrated
(MuRIL full-FT 0.0702, mBERT 0.0657, TeluguBERT 0.0634,
MuRIL LoRA 0.0540), and logistic regression is 0.0680. The
pattern is consistent with the literature: boosted trees and
linear models with proper loss are better calibrated than overparameterized neural networks [28]. For a deployment that
thresholds probabilities or abstains, this matters more than a
0.4-point macro-F1 difference.

VII. D ISCUSSION
A. Why Does the Linear Model Win?
Five mechanisms, each supported by a separate experiment,
explain the result.
1) The task is local and lexical. Comments have median
8 tokens and median 48 characters. Polarity is carried
by a small set of high-frequency cues (bagundi, chala,
keka, waste, bore). A linear model over word unigrams

learns these cues directly; a transformer must learn them
through many layers and a much larger hypothesis space.
2) Character n-grams absorb orthographic variation.
Romanized Telugu has many spellings for the same
word (bagundi, bavundhi, baagundhi). Character 2–5grams share substrings across variants; WordPiece splits
them into near-disjoint pieces. The feature ablation (char
0.8276, union 0.8324) and the tokenizer experiment
(0.7863 → 0.7945) quantify both sides of this mechanism.
3) Small data favors high bias. With 27,670 training rows
and a noisy label ﬂoor, the variance of a 238M-parameter
model is a liability. Full ﬁne-tuning’s training loss falls
from 0.886 to 0.242 while validation peaks at epoch
4 (0.8255) and never recovers—textbook overﬁtting.
LoRA reduces variance but also reduces capacity to
0.59M parameters, and it lands at 0.8165.
4) Pretraining register mismatch. MuRIL’s Romanized
pretraining is formal and rule-based (Wikipedia, Dakshina), whereas Tenglish comments are informal and

phonetically spelled. Full ﬁne-tuning can shift the representation; a low-rank update cannot shift it enough.
The 1.71-point LoRA/full-FT gap is the price of that

5) More parameters are redundant once the cues are
represented. The capacity–accuracy correlation is weak
(ρ = 0.306) and non-signiﬁcant. The 279M-parameter
XLM-R LoRA (0.8103) is below the 238M-parameter
MuRIL LoRA (0.8165), and both are below a 50.7Kcoefﬁcient linear model. Capacity is not the bottleneck.
B. A Linguistic Reading: The Matrix-Language Frame
Myers-Scotton’s Matrix Language Frame (MLF) model
[29] distinguishes the matrix language, which supplies the
morphosyntactic frame, from embedded-language islands. In
Tenglish, Telugu typically supplies the frame and English
supplies content islands, while sentiment is carried by a
small set of content morphemes (bagundi ‘good’, waste, keka
‘superb’) and system morphemes (-ledu ‘not’, kaadhu ‘is
not’, kani ‘but’). These cues are morpheme-local and largely
order-insensitive: their polarity does not depend on long-range
syntactic composition. This is exactly the regime in which a
unigram/character model has the right inductive bias, and it
is consistent with the ﬁnding that word bigrams add noise
rather than signal. MLF is a grammatical theory, not a theory
of model capacity; it does not by itself explain why a 238Mparameter model fails to beat a linear one. It does explain why
the linear model’s hypothesis space is well matched to the cue
structure, which is the ﬁrst half of the explanation.
C. The Capacity Illusion
We use the term capacity illusion for the inferential error of
attributing a leaderboard gap to model capacity when the gap

is produced by leakage, tokenization, or tuning. In our matrix,
the raw ordering “238M > 50K” survives, but its magnitude
collapses from a reported 5% accuracy improvement to a
non-signiﬁcant 0.43-point macro-F1 gap once (i) the split is
group-disjoint, (ii) tokenization is tested as a separate factor
rather than confounded with architecture, and (iii) the linear
baseline is tuned with the same search budget. The illusion is
not that transformers are bad; it is that the comparison was
not controlled. A benchmark that reports only the winning
transformer, without a tuned linear baseline and a leakage
audit, cannot distinguish “the model learned the task” from
“the split leaked the task”.
D. Relation to Published Claims
Rayala and Seshadri report a 5% accuracy and 6% F1 improvement of transformers over baselines on CMTE-NITANP
[2]. Our leak-free comparison does not reproduce a gap of that
size. There are three non-exclusive explanations: their split
may differ from ours; their baseline may be untuned; or their
transformer may use a different adaptation regime. We cannot
adjudicate without their exact split and baseline numbers,
which are not available in the accessible record. What we can
say is that on a split with zero exact Jaccard≥ 0.8 train–test
pairs, with a tuned linear baseline and a frozen hyperparameter
protocol, the transformer advantage is 0.43 macro-F1 points
and is not signiﬁcant. Any claim of a 5–6% advantage should
therefore be accompanied by a split audit and a tuned linear
baseline.
E. How to Beat the Benchmark
If the goal is to exceed the best published number rather
than to explain it, the evidence points to four levers, in order
of expected value:

IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AFFECTIVE COMPUTING (UNDER REVIEW)

1) Ensemble the full-ﬁne-tuned transformer with the
linear model. The two models make different errors
(negative vs. positive recall), and a soft-voting ensemble
of diverse strong models is the cheapest lever likely to
add a meaningful margin.
2) Full ﬁne-tuning at max_len=96. Truncation falls from
3.15% to 0.17%, and full ﬁne-tuning has the capacity to
exploit the extra context. Run three seeds and report the
mean.
3) Tune the full-ﬁne-tuning learning rate. The sensitivity
study shows that 2 × 10−4 is harmful for LoRA; full
ﬁne-tuning at 2 × 10−5 was chosen from a small grid.
A three-point grid (1, 2, 3 × 10−5 ) with early stopping
is the next step.
4) Try XLM-R full ﬁne-tuning on a larger GPU. XLMR has the strongest multilingual coverage in our LoRA
matrix, but its 279M parameters at full ﬁne-tuning exceed the 4 GB budget. A 16 GB T4 makes this feasible.
Any claim of beating the published number must use the same
split and metric, report conﬁdence intervals, and audit the split.
Otherwise the new number is not comparable.

F. Recommendations for Practitioners
Always report a tuned TF-IDF linear baseline. It is fast,
strong, and calibrated; on this task it is within noise of
the best transformer.
• Audit the split with exact Jaccard matching, not only
MinHash. MinHash ﬂagged 203 test rows (5.87%); the
exact audit found 345 train–test pairs at or above 0.8,
so a MinHash pass alone is not a substitute for exact
veriﬁcation.
• Prefer character n-grams or a word+character union when
spelling is variable; word bigrams can hurt.
• If using a transformer, prefer full ﬁne-tuning over lowrank adaptation when the domain mismatch is large and
the GPU allows it; LoRA is a compute compromise, not
a free lunch.
• Report calibration and robustness alongside accuracy. The
best macro-F1 model is not the best-calibrated or the most
robust model.

VIII. T HREATS TO VALIDITY
a) Internal validity.: Split B is constructed with a speciﬁc clustering threshold (0.8) and shingle deﬁnition (word
3-grams). The audit is stable across 0.7–0.9, but a different
shingle deﬁnition could produce a different partition. The label
noise ﬂoor (115 conﬂicting clusters, 314 rows) is small but
non-zero and affects all models equally.
b) External validity.: The study covers one language pair
and one corpus. CMTE-NITANP is a comment corpus, not
Twitter; the lexical cues and spelling conventions may differ
on other platforms. The cross-corpus experiment is a partial
check, but CMTET has a different label scheme and domain,
so it is not a clean domain-shift estimate.

11

c) Construct validity.: Macro-F1 treats the three classes
equally, which is appropriate for the near-balanced test set but
may not reﬂect deployment costs. Neutral is the hardest class;
a deployment that cares disproportionately about neutral would
need a different metric.
d) Statistical validity.: Full ﬁne-tuning has one seed
because of its cost. Its conﬁdence interval is therefore a test-set
bootstrap interval, not a seed-to-seed interval. The linear model
has ﬁve seeds but zero variance because it is deterministic; its
interval is also a test-set bootstrap interval. The comparison is
paired and uses McNemar, which is appropriate for a single
ﬁxed test set, but it does not capture seed variability of
the transformer. A three-seed full-ﬁne-tuning run is the main
missing experiment.
e) Compute validity.: The 4 GB GPU forces batch 8 with
accumulation and 8-bit AdamW for full ﬁne-tuning. A larger
GPU might change the optimal batch and learning rate. The
sensitivity study partially controls for this, but it does not
eliminate the constraint.
IX. C ONCLUSION
We presented a leakage-audited benchmark of 21 conﬁgurations and 85 runs for Romanized Telugu–English sentiment
on CMTE-NITANP. On a group-disjoint split with zero exact
Jaccard≥ 0.8 train–test pairs, a 238M-parameter fully ﬁnetuned MuRIL reaches 83.36 macro-F1; a TF-IDF logistic
regression with no pretrained parameters reaches 82.93 (83.24
with a word+character union in a single-seed ablation). The
difference is not statistically signiﬁcant. Parameter count does
not predict accuracy, LoRA adapters trail the linear model,
word bigrams are worse than unigrams, and WordPiece tokenization does not close the gap. The transformer advantage
reported on this corpus is therefore consistent with a capacity
illusion produced by uncontrolled splits, tokenization, and
tuning. The practical recipe is unglamorous: audit the split,
tune a linear baseline, use character n-grams, and only then
decide whether a transformer’s small and fragile gain is worth
300× the compute.
